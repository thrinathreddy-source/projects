Path B

The lens device

A student's reading companion: point it at a textbook page or a sign, get a translated overlay. Same open language stack, different last mile.

Revised below. The BOM this started with assumed a birdbath combiner + bare micro-OLED panel someone bonds themselves. Turns out that's not how real in-lens displays get made — even Brilliant Labs' Frame, the closest open-hardware match to G1, is factory-sealed with the OLED optically bonded to the prism at the factory; its own docs warn that opening it means "destructive disassembly." Sub-100-micron optical alignment isn't a home-assembly step. So the real version of "open + doesn't look bad" means: buy the optical engine as one sealed, pre-bonded cartridge, and build everything else — frame, battery, driver board — as the actual open, snap-together kit around it.

What the optical engine actually costs — and why "monochrome" is the answer, not "cheaper distributor"

The first version of this said $499 like it was the answer, sourced from one full-color module at a US prototyping distributor. That was the wrong reference class entirely — not because of markup, but because full-color and monochrome are different technologies with very different costs, and $150–300 is a monochrome-tier target.

Full-color, JBD's own cost-reduction target"$20/eye"Verified quote from JBD's CEO — but stated as 2–5 years away from being real. Not available today at any volume.
Monochrome (what G1 and Rokid actually use)Likely $20–50/eye already, at real production volumeTriangulated, not directly quoted: Rokid Glasses retails its entire device — two monochrome micro-LED engines, a full Qualcomm AR1 SoC, 12MP camera, frame — for $499 total. That math only works if each engine costs a small fraction of that.

So: $150–300 for a monocular monochrome engine is a genuinely plausible target — probably even on the high side — if it's sourced at real production volume from a supplier who's actually shipping it. That's a short list: JBD is described industry-wide as "the only company to realize monetization of MicroLED micro-display," and is the confirmed supplier behind G1 and TCL's RayNeo X3 Pro. Their published dev kits (Hummingbird Mini I/II, monochrome) don't list retail prices — getting the real number for a small production run means contacting JBD directly, not guessing further from here. That's the one concrete next step that would turn "plausible" into "confirmed."

"Just simple text, no color" is not a compromise here — it's the actual product category. JBD's own marketing for the Hummingbird line is explicitly for "lightweight AR glasses," and G1 (text, navigation, notifications) is the real shipping proof that this spec is enough for a genuinely useful device. Full color is the harder, more expensive, not-ready-today path for a use case you don't need. One correction to an earlier assumption, though: checking JBD's own dev kit page, its accessories list an HDMI cable and USB adapter — meaning even the monochrome engine's dev kit expects real video-rate input, not a simple SPI/I2C text buffer an ESP32 could drive directly. So the video-capable hub (~$12–15, the Pi Zero 2 W class board) most likely still belongs in the BOM even for text-only. What a real production device does internally to get G1's long battery life is probably a more direct, efficient interface than the dev kit's HDMI path — but that's the kind of integration JBD does with an actual manufacturing partner, not something visible from a public dev-kit page.

Stripped to essentials — cutting what isn't the actual ask

"Lower" has two honest levers left, beyond hoping the display gets cheaper. Both mean removing things, not better sourcing:

CutReasoningSavings
Drop the standalone video hub (~$12)The whole video/compute-capable SoC was for a hub-free, fully offline mode. Almost everyone this is for already owns some phone — India's mobile penetration is high even among low-income users. Let the phone render frames and stream them over BLE/Wi-Fi Direct instead; the glasses only need enough to receive and relay to the driver board, which is a simpler, cheaper job than full on-device rendering.−$10–12 (a simple relay chip instead, ~$2–3, estimate not a sourced part)

At the low end of the display range, that's real, added movement — not a trick, an actual smaller device that does less (relies on a phone you already own, drops OCR, drops gesture/auto-brightness). The display line is still the one lever left that could move further, and still needs a real JBD quote to confirm rather than assume.

How light — reasoned from real shipping products, not guessed

DeviceWeightWhat it's carrying

Rokid Glasses48gBinocular monochrome plus a full Qualcomm AR1 SoC and 12MP camera — the extra ~8–9g over G1 is exactly the cost of carrying onboard compute and a camera

The pattern across all three: frame material and mechanical structure (hinges, temple arms, nose bridge) is the weight floor no matter what, but everything carried inside the frame is optional weight. This build is already the minimalist end of that spectrum — monocular (one waveguide engine, not two) and phone-tethered (no onboard SoC, no camera, matching G1's approach rather than Rokid's). Reasoned from G1's own <40g binocular budget: removing one entire optical engine and driver plausibly saves 8–10g on its own, before even counting the camera/SoC G1 already doesn't carry.

Target: ~26–32g — lighter than G1, on the strength of being monocular where G1 is binocular

One more real lever, borrowed directly from G1's own design: it ships with a charging case that has its own 2,000mAh battery, so the glasses themselves only need enough onboard battery for hours between case top-ups, not multi-day standalone capacity. The same trick applies here — keep the onboard battery small (which is already what the ~$3 battery line item in the BOM assumes) and put the bulk capacity in a pocket-sized case instead of on someone's face. That's how G1 gets away with under 40 grams carrying real electronics, and it's copyable, not proprietary.

How it could actually look

A fictional reference point like EDITH (Tony Stark's glasses from Spider-Man: Far From Home) isn't a fair engineering bar — it's CGI: light-blue lenses, silver frame, no bonded optics, no battery, no hinges taking real mechanical stress. But the style it's evoking — sleek, minimal, could pass as fashion eyewear — is a real, already-proven target. Reviewers on G1 don't say "pretty good for a tech product"; they say "genuinely normal, wear-them-to-a-job-interview glasses." The only tells they found: a faint rectangular waveguide visible up close (easily mistaken for progressive bifocals) and slightly chunky capsules at the temple tips housing electronics.

This build has two concrete reasons to do better than G1 on exactly those two tells, not just match it:

One waveguide tell, not two

G1 shows the faint rectangular waveguide in both lenses (binocular). This build is monocular — one lens is genuinely plain glass or a normal prescription lens, zero tech, zero tell. Only one side has anything to notice at all.

Slimmer temple tips

G1's "dead giveaway" temple capsules house its onboard electronics. This build carries less there — no camera, no heavy SoC (phone-tethered), same small-battery-plus-case trick G1 already uses. Less to hide means a real shot at thinner temple tips than G1's.

Frame material doesn't need to be exotic to get there, either — G1 uses magnesium/titanium alloy, but plenty of genuinely stylish real eyewear (including Rokid's temples) is molded TR90 or polycarbonate, which is cheap, light, and comes in real colors/finishes. And because the optical cartridge is a small sealed module, the same core engine could sit behind multiple open frame designs — round, square, whatever — the same way G1 ships as both G1A and G1B. The honest answer: closer to G1 than to EDITH, but with real reasons to be even less noticeable than G1 — and G1 already clears "normal glasses" by most reviewers' account.

This is a synthesized estimate, not a quote. The non-display parts (MCU, camera, mic, sensors, battery, mechanical) are grounded in real component pricing sourced earlier this conversation and are unlikely to move much. The display line is the one that matters: $150–300 is a real, evidence-triangulated target for a monochrome engine at production volume (see above), not a number pulled from a distributor listing. It is still not a confirmed quote — JBD is the concrete next contact for that. Two numbers to keep separate: this monochrome target (~$190–230 total, matches what you asked for) versus a full-color build using the DisplayModule-class part, which stays at ~$650–700 regardless of volume tier, because full-color's cost floor is years out per JBD's own roadmap. Monochrome is the affordable path; full-color is the expensive one, and that's a spec choice, not a sourcing problem.

What a normal person actually pays

Manufacturing cost and end-user price are two different numbers. Closing that gap for someone who isn't wealthy is a pricing and funding decision, not an engineering one — here's that decision laid out honestly, at today's rate (₹95 ≈ $1), using the stripped, phone-tethered BOM above (~$185–218 manufacturing cost, camera and hub removed).

Stripping the hub and camera moved the honest at-cost floor from ₹23,000–25,000 down to roughly ₹18,000–20,000. That's real movement, not a trick — a smaller device that leans on a phone you already own instead of carrying its own. It's still a significant sum, not "walk in and buy it" money for most of the people this was actually built for. Getting further below that isn't a components problem anymore; it needs one of these, all with real precedent in India specifically:

EMI financing

₹19,000 at 0%-interest "no-cost EMI" (standard for electronics in India already) becomes under ₹1,600/month for a year. Doesn't lower the real cost, but changes whether someone can actually afford it — this alone closes most of the practical gap without needing a subsidy at all.

CSR mandate (real law)

Companies Act 2013, Section 135 legally requires large Indian companies to spend 2% of net profit on CSR — education and assistive technology already qualify. This is a real, large, existing pool of money, not a hypothetical grant to chase.

Government procurement

States have already run free-laptop and free-tablet schemes for students (Tamil Nadu, Punjab, UP). A device this framed as educational/assistive equity has a real precedent path into that kind of bulk, subsidized distribution — sold to government at cost, given to the end user free or near-free.

Cross-subsidy

Sell at the lean-D2C price (~₹24,000–27,000) to willing urban/institutional buyers, use that margin to fund free or steeply discounted units for the farm-worker/student users this was actually built for. Real precedent in Indian social enterprises, not a novel idea.

None of these are things I can set up from here — they're real-world funding and distribution work for a person or organization to pursue. The one lever still left that could move the ₹18,000–20,000 floor itself, rather than who pays it, is the display module: it's still a triangulated target, not a confirmed quote, and a real JBD conversation at genuine committed volume (a government or NGO order, not one person buying one unit) is the only thing that could move it further. Everything else in this device is already a normal, sourced parts list.

What actually keeps this open

The point of open hardware here isn't ideology — it's that nobody should have to pay a monthly fee just to keep hearing their own language back from a device they already bought.

Schematics + CAD

KiCad source and STL/STEP files published under CERN-OHL-W — anyone can fabricate without a royalty.

Firmware

Built on ESP-IDF / Arduino core, licensed Apache-2.0 or GPL. No vendor SDK, no locked bootloader.

Language models

AI4Bharat's open-weight ASR, MT and TTS models run entirely on hardware you own — no per-call fee.

Cloud fallback

Bhashini, the Government of India's public language API, is free infrastructure — a safety net, never a requirement.
